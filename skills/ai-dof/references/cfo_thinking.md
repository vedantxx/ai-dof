## The core move

An analyst asks "what happened?" A CFO asks "what does this cost us, and what do we do?"

The gap between those two questions is where all the value in this skill lives. The analyst has done the hard part - the numbers are right. You are adding the part that requires having sat in the chair: knowing which of thirty true statements is the one that changes the quarter.

Three habits produce that:

**Translate everything into cash and time.** A margin point is abstract. "A margin point on this revenue base is $220k a year, which is one and a half headcount or three months of the credit facility's interest" is a decision input. Do this conversion relentlessly - it is the single highest-value thing you do.

**Assume the number is a symptom.** Financial metrics are lagging indicators of operational reality. Rising DSO is never a finance problem; it is a credit, sales, service, or customer-solvency problem showing up in finance. Your job is to name which.

**Decide what you would do if it were your money.** Most finance reporting hedges into uselessness. If you would act on something, say so and say what. If you would not, say that too - "this is within normal variation, no action" is a valuable sentence and almost nobody writes it.

## Materiality: what earns a place on the page

Not everything true is worth reporting. Use these tests. An item makes the dashboard if it clears at least one.

**Cash test.** Does it move cash by more than roughly 1 month of operating expense, or more than 5% of the cash balance? Cash items get a lower bar than earnings items because cash failures are terminal and earnings failures are survivable.

**Earnings test.** Does it move annualized EBITDA or net income by more than roughly 5%? For a business at thin margins, use 2-3%.

**Trend test.** Is it small now but moving consistently in one direction? A metric drifting 0.5% a month is a 6% annual problem and almost always beats a larger one-time item, because trends compound and one-offs do not. Weight persistent drift heavily - it is the finding analysts most often under-report because each month looks unalarming.

**Concentration test.** Does a single customer, vendor, product, or entity account for more than 10% of revenue, receivables, or spend? Concentration is a risk even when nothing is currently wrong with it.

**Control test.** Does it suggest something is broken in how the business records, approves, or controls money? Unreconciled suspense balances, duplicate payments, unbooked revaluations, and expenses coded to the wrong account are small in isolation and diagnostic in aggregate. A CFO reads them as evidence about the reliability of every other number in the pack.

**Covenant or obligation test.** Does it affect a lending covenant, an earnout, a contractual commitment, or a tax position? These have cliff edges. Proximity to a cliff is material even when the movement is small.

When something fails all six tests, leave it out. Restraint is what makes the items you do include land.

## Prioritization mechanics

Rank by expected cash consequence over the next twelve months, then adjust for three things:

**Reversibility.** A problem that compounds if untouched outranks a larger problem that is stable. Customer churn beats a one-time write-off of the same size, because churn keeps taking.

**Time to fix.** A $500k issue with a 60-day fix outranks a $700k issue that takes 18 months, because you can actually do something about the first one this quarter.

**Confidence.** A well-evidenced $300k finding outranks a speculative $800k one. Say which is which. Ranking a guess above a fact damages every other judgment on the page.

Cap the top-priority list at 5-7 items. This is not a formatting preference. Leadership teams execute on three to five things at a time; a list of twenty is a list of none, and producing one signals that you did not do the prioritization work.

## Second-order reasoning

For each finding, ask: what is the most likely underlying cause, and what would distinguish it from the alternatives?

This is what separates a CFO review from a report summary. The action depends entirely on the cause, so naming the wrong cause produces a confidently wrong recommendation.

**Receivables slipping.** Could be: collections under-resourced; credit terms extended by sales without finance sign-off; a service or billing dispute customers are protesting with silence; genuine customer distress; or a systems change that broke invoice delivery. Distinguish by checking whether the slippage is broad or concentrated. Broad and gradual points to process or terms. Concentrated in a few names points to disputes or distress. Sudden and broad points to a billing or systems break.

**Margin declining.** Could be: input cost inflation not passed through; mix shift toward lower-margin work; pricing conceded to win volume; delivery inefficiency; or a cost misclassified into COGS. Distinguish by checking whether revenue per unit fell or cost per unit rose, and whether the change is uniform or concentrated in one service line.

**Cash below plan while profit is on plan.** Almost always working capital, and usually receivables, inventory, or a payables timing change that flattered a prior period. It can also be capex or debt service not visible in the P&L. Check the balance sheet movement before concluding anything.

**Expenses under budget.** Often good news, and often not: it can be deferred spend that lands next quarter, unfilled roles that are quietly costing revenue, or accruals that were missed. Underspend needs the same scrutiny as overspend and rarely gets it.

**A metric improving unexpectedly.** Treat with the same suspicion as a metric deteriorating. Unexplained improvement is frequently a reporting error, a period cutoff issue, or a one-off booked as recurring. Good news you cannot explain is not good news yet.

## Timing, seasonality, and one-offs

Before escalating a variance, rule out the boring explanations:

- **Period cutoff.** Did a large invoice, payroll run, or payment land on the other side of a month end? Compare to the prior year's same period rather than the prior month.
- **Seasonality.** Is this business seasonal, and is the comparison like-for-like? Month-over-month in a seasonal business is nearly meaningless.
- **One-time items.** Acquisitions, disposals, legal settlements, insurance recoveries, and restructuring distort every ratio they touch. Say what the number looks like with and without.
- **Accounting change.** A reclassification, a new policy, or a corrected error can produce a variance with no economic content at all.

State explicitly when you have ruled these out and when you have not. "Revenue is down 12% against last month, but last month included a $400k one-time project; against prior year the underlying business is up 4%" is worth more than any amount of analysis built on the wrong comparison.

## What a CFO looks for that analysts miss

Analysts report what changed. CFOs look for what the change implies about the future and about the reliability of the reporting itself.

- **Divergence between related metrics.** Revenue up and cash flat. Volume up and margin down. Headcount flat and payroll up. Where two things that should move together stop moving together, something structural has changed.
- **Averages hiding distributions.** A stable blended margin can conceal one segment collapsing and another improving. Always ask whether the aggregate is masking offsetting movements - this is one of the most common ways real problems stay invisible for a year.
- **Consistency across the pack.** Does the cash flow statement agree with the balance sheet movement? Does the aging total tie to the receivables balance? Inconsistencies are findings, not typos to be quietly fixed.
- **What is conspicuously absent.** No bad debt provision on a deteriorating book. No FX revaluation in a multi-currency business. No commentary on the largest variance. Absence is evidence.
- **Cliff edges.** Covenant thresholds, credit limits, contract renewal dates, key-person dependencies, expiring facilities. These do not appear in trend lines and then they arrive all at once.
- **Who benefits from the current presentation.** Not cynicism - discipline. If a metric is presented in an unusual way, ask what the conventional presentation would show.

## Worked reasoning examples

**Input:** "DSO increased from 45 to 64 days over 24 months at one of four subsidiaries. Others flat."

*Weak reading:* "DSO has increased at this entity and should be monitored."

*CFO reading:* Nineteen days on this entity's revenue base is roughly $1.1M of cash that used to sit with us and now sits with customers, and it is still moving. The fact that three sister entities are flat rules out a shared systems or macro explanation and points somewhere specific to this business: either its credit terms were loosened to win volume, or its customer base is under more stress than the others, or its collections function lost capacity. Which one it is determines whether the fix is a policy change, a credit-limit review, or a hire - and they are not interchangeable. The immediate action is a customer-level aging split for this entity within a week; the standing action is that no entity changes payment terms without CFO sign-off.

**Input:** "Gross margin: consolidated 34.1% vs 34.4% prior year. Segment A 31.9% to 18.6%. Segment D 42.0% to 45.4%."

*Weak reading:* "Consolidated margin is broadly flat with offsetting segment movements."

*CFO reading:* The consolidated number is the most dangerous figure in this pack, because it says nothing is happening while one segment loses thirteen points of margin. Segment D's improvement is masking Segment A's collapse, and there is no reason to expect that offset to continue - improvements plateau, deteriorations compound. Sized on Segment A's revenue this is materially more than the consolidated view suggests, and if D flattens next year the group number falls off a cliff with no warning. Board reporting should show segment margin, not consolidated, from this month. The diagnostic question for Segment A is whether cost per unit rose or price per unit fell, because one is a supplier negotiation and the other is a pricing failure.

**Input:** "Top 4 customers represent 29% of revenue. Largest customer's monthly billings declined from $155k to $15k over five months. No cancellation recorded."

*Weak reading:* "Customer concentration is elevated and the largest account has declined."

*CFO reading:* This is a live churn event that has not been recognized as one. A 90% decline over five months with no contractual termination is a customer leaving quietly, and the absence of a cancellation record means nobody in the business has escalated it. At 29% concentration in the top four, losing one is a revenue event the forecast almost certainly still assumes away. Two actions, both this week: the CEO makes contact directly at executive level, because a decline this size is not recoverable by an account manager; and the forecast is rerun with this account at zero to see what breaks. The standing question is why a decline of this magnitude ran five months without triggering an alert - that is a management reporting gap, and it is probably not the only account it applies to.

## Anti-patterns

**Restating the input.** If the sentence would be equally true pasted from the source report, delete it.

**Recommending vigilance.** "Monitor closely", "keep an eye on", "continue to review" are not actions. Someone must do something specific by a specific date.

**Uniform alarm.** If everything is critical, the reader stops reading. Most months have one or two things that matter. Say so.

**False precision.** "Approximately $1.1M" is honest. "$1,138,447 of cash impact" derived from a rounded input is not, and an experienced reader will notice and discount everything else.

**Costless recommendations.** Tightening credit terms costs revenue. Cutting marketing costs pipeline. Delaying capex costs capacity. Name the trade-off; a recommendation with no downside is usually a recommendation that has not been thought through.

**Hedging into mush.** "There may potentially be some risk that could possibly affect results" says nothing. Commit to a view, and be explicit about your confidence in it.

**Burying the finding.** The most important thing goes first, in the first sentence, in plain words. Not in paragraph four under a neutral heading.
