# Risk, Collection, Cash Flow, and Concentration Frameworks

Four assessment lenses. Use the severity language exactly as defined - the value of a recurring CFO review is that "High" means the same thing every month.

## Risk assessment framework

### Categories

Assign every risk to one category. The category determines who owns it and how fast it escalates.

| Category | What it covers | Typical owner |
|---|---|---|
| **Liquidity** | Cash runway, facility headroom, working capital absorption, payment timing | CFO |
| **Credit** | Customer solvency, collectability, bad debt adequacy, credit limits | Controller / CFO |
| **Earnings** | Margin, pricing, cost structure, mix, volume | CFO / COO |
| **Concentration** | Customer, vendor, product, geography, key-person dependency | CEO / CFO |
| **Covenant** | Lending ratios, contractual obligations, earnouts, tax positions | CFO |
| **Operational** | Delivery capability, capacity, systems, supply | COO |
| **Control** | Process failures, unreconciled items, duplicates, coding errors, segregation | Controller |
| **Reporting** | Data integrity, recognition, classification, disclosure | Controller / CFO |

Control and Reporting risks are frequently under-weighted because they are individually small. Weight them by what they imply: if expenses are landing in the wrong accounts and suspense balances are not clearing, every number in the pack carries an unquantified error bar, and that is a finding in its own right.

### Severity

Severity is the combination of impact and likelihood, adjusted for proximity. Something certain and near outranks something larger and distant.

**Critical** - Threatens solvency, covenant compliance, or a business-critical relationship. Requires action this week. Escalate to CEO and board. Examples: liquidity below one month of operating expense; covenant breach projected within two quarters; loss of a customer above 15% of revenue; discovery of a material misstatement.

**High** - Material impact on cash or earnings, currently deteriorating, and will worsen without intervention. Action within 30 days. Escalate to CEO. Examples: a segment losing several margin points a year; receivables aging deteriorating in one part of the book; a top-five customer showing distress signals.

**Medium** - Real but contained, or material but stable. Action this quarter. Manage at CFO level. Examples: a vendor cost creeping above inflation; a modest concentration build; an underperforming cost line.

**Low** - Worth recording so the trend is visible, but not worth management attention this period. Note and move on. Do not pad the dashboard with these.

### Escalation triggers

Escalate to Critical regardless of size when any of these are true, because each has a cliff edge rather than a slope:

- Cash runway under 90 days
- Covenant headroom under 10%
- A single customer above 15% of revenue showing decline or distress
- Evidence of fraud, misappropriation, or deliberate misstatement
- Receivables over 120 days exceeding the bad debt provision
- Any finding that means previously reported figures were wrong

## Collection risk framework

### The question that matters

Not "how old is this balance" but "will we get paid, and what does it cost us to find out."

Age is the most visible signal and the least diagnostic. A 90-day balance from a customer who always pays at 90 is a terms problem. A 45-day balance from a customer who has always paid at 30 is an early warning. Read the change in behaviour, not the absolute number.

### Scoring a balance

Assess five dimensions. Weight them roughly in this order:

1. **Payment behaviour change** - Is this customer paying materially slower than their own history? The strongest predictor of non-payment, and the one aging reports hide.
2. **Age relative to terms** - Days past due, not days outstanding. A 60-day balance on Net 60 is current.
3. **Balance size** - Absolute exposure. Concentration of risk in a few names is worse than the same total spread widely.
4. **Dispute status** - Disputed balances are a service problem wearing a finance costume. They do not respond to collections pressure and often signal a relationship at risk.
5. **Customer health** - Declining volumes, missed commitments, changed contacts, requests for extended terms. Volume decline plus payment slowdown at the same customer is the clearest distress signal in receivables and warrants immediate escalation.

### Tiers

**Critical** - Over 120 days past due, or any material balance where the customer has stopped communicating, or a balance exceeding the customer's credit limit. Assume impairment until proven otherwise. Action: stop further credit, escalate to executive contact, get a written payment commitment or begin recovery within 14 days.

**High** - 90-120 days past due, or a large balance decelerating sharply against its own history, or a disputed balance older than 60 days. Action: named owner, weekly contact cadence, resolution path with dates.

**Medium** - 60-90 days past due, or a moderate balance drifting. Action: structured follow-up, review credit terms at next renewal.

**Low** - Within terms or marginally past. Standard process, no escalation.

### Prioritizing collection effort

Rank by **recoverable cash per unit of effort**, not by balance size. The largest balance is often the one already in a process; the highest-yield call is usually a mid-sized balance from a solvent customer where nobody has picked up the phone.

Practical ordering:
1. Large balances, solvent customer, no dispute, simply not chased - the fastest cash in the building.
2. Disputed balances with a clear resolution path - resolving the dispute releases the cash and repairs the relationship.
3. Large balances, distress signals - lower probability, but the exposure justifies senior attention now rather than later.
4. Aged balances from customers who have gone quiet - assess for write-off; effort here is usually better spent elsewhere, and pretending otherwise inflates the forecast.

### Provision adequacy

Compare the bad debt provision to the aged book. Two failures to look for:

- **Under-reserved where risk is highest.** If the entity or segment with the worst aging carries the lowest provision rate, the provision is being set by habit rather than by risk. Say so plainly - it means reported earnings are overstated.
- **Aged balances exceeding the provision entirely.** If balances over 120 days exceed the total provision, the write-off is already incurred and simply not recognized yet.

## Cash flow review framework

### The four questions

**1. How much cash, and for how long?** Runway in months against current burn. Under 6 months is a standing board item; under 3 is Critical regardless of what else the pack says.

**2. Is profit converting to cash?** Compare earnings to operating cash flow. Persistent divergence means working capital is absorbing the profit - receivables growing faster than revenue, inventory building, or payables that were stretched and are now normalizing. Profit that does not convert is not yet real.

**3. What is committed?** Debt service, capex commitments, tax payments, earnouts, seasonal working capital swings. Cash that looks available and is already spoken for is the most common cause of a liquidity surprise.

**4. Is the forecast credible?** Check it against recent actuals. If the last three forecasts were optimistic by 15%, this one probably is too. State the historical accuracy alongside the forecast - a forecast without a track record is a hope, and presenting it as a plan is a failure of the finance function.

### Working capital diagnostics

- **Receivables growing faster than revenue** - collections or credit deterioration; cash tied up in customers
- **Payables growing faster than costs** - may be deliberate cash management, may be inability to pay; determine which, because the second is a solvency signal
- **Inventory growing faster than COGS** - demand miss or purchasing discipline
- **Cash conversion cycle lengthening** - each additional day costs roughly one day of operating expense in working capital

### Forecast presentation

Give the reader a range, not a point. Expected case and at-risk case, with what separates them stated explicitly. Point forecasts get treated as commitments and then missed.

Flag every material assumption. If the forecast assumes the largest customer keeps paying at historical speed and that customer is showing distress, the forecast is wrong and should be labelled accordingly.

## Concentration risk framework

### Thresholds

| Exposure | Threshold | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Single customer, revenue | >10% | Disclose and monitor |
| Single customer, revenue | >20% | Strategic dependency; board-level |
| Top 5 customers, revenue | >40% | Structurally concentrated |
| Single customer, receivables | >15% | Credit concentration |
| Single vendor, cost of sales | >20% | Supply and pricing dependency |
| Single entity or segment, EBITDA | >50% | Group depends on one engine |

Concentration is not automatically bad - many excellent businesses are concentrated by design. It is bad when it is unrecognized, unmanaged, or unaccompanied by a plan for the day it ends.

### Vendor concentration and fragmented spend

Vendor concentration frequently hides in plain sight because spend is fragmented across name variants, legal entities, or subsidiaries of the same parent. Where the input suggests this, note that consolidated spend may be materially understated and that a true vendor ranking requires merging related names. A supplier who looks like the eighth-largest may be the second, which changes the negotiating position entirely.

### Churn signals

Revenue concentration matters most in combination with churn risk. These signals, especially in combination, indicate a customer is leaving before anyone says so:

- Billings declining consistently over three or more periods without a contractual change
- Payment behaviour slowing at the same time as volume declining
- Service mix narrowing - the customer keeping only the commodity work
- Contact changes, longer response times, renewal discussions that keep slipping

A customer above 10% of revenue showing two or more of these is Critical, not High. The revenue is already leaving; the only question is whether the forecast has caught up.

## Severity reference table

| Level | Trigger | Action window | Escalate to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Solvency, covenant, fraud, or loss of a >15% relationship | This week | Board / CEO |
| **High** | Material and deteriorating; will worsen untreated | 30 days | CEO |
| **Medium** | Real but contained, or material but stable | This quarter | CFO |
| **Low** | Trend worth recording only | Note only | Controller |

Use these labels verbatim in the dashboard so severity is comparable across periods.
