# Executive Communication Standards

How to write so a CEO acts on it.

## The audience

A CEO, board member, owner, or finance leader with fifteen minutes and other things on their mind. They are not reading for completeness. They are reading to find out whether anything requires them, and what.

Write for someone who will read the first paragraph carefully, skim the headings, and stop at whatever alarms them. Structure accordingly: conclusion first, evidence second, detail third.

## The five standards

**1. Lead with the conclusion.**

Not the background, not the methodology, not what the data covers. The answer.

> Weak: "This review covers accounts receivable performance for the period, with attention to aging and collection trends across the four operating entities."
>
> Strong: "Two things need attention this month. Cascade's receivables have slipped 19 days and are still moving, and the largest customer at Northwind has effectively stopped buying without anyone recording a cancellation. Everything else is within normal range."

**2. Quantify in currency and time.**

Adjectives are unfalsifiable and therefore ignorable. Numbers are decisions.

> Weak: "Receivables performance has deteriorated significantly."
>
> Strong: "Days-to-pay went from 45 to 64 over two years - roughly $1.1M of cash that used to be ours and is now sitting with customers, growing by about $45k a month."

**3. Add meaning, never restate.**

Every sentence must contain something the source report did not.

> Weak: "Gross margin at Cascade fell from 31.9% to 18.6%."
>
> Strong: "Cascade has lost 13 points of gross margin in two years. Apex improving at the same time has kept the consolidated number nearly flat, which is why this has not surfaced before - and there is no reason to expect that offset to hold, because improvements plateau and deteriorations compound."

**4. Own the uncertainty.**

Confidence you have not earned is worse than an acknowledged gap. Say what you know, what you infer, and what would settle it.

> Weak: "The margin decline is due to fuel costs."
>
> Strong: "Fuel is the largest single contributor, but the data does not separate rate from volume. If unit cost is the driver this is a supplier negotiation; if it is mix it is a pricing problem. A fuel spend per mile split would settle it and takes a day."

**5. Cut anything that would not change a decision.**

Length signals effort; brevity signals judgment. A clean month should produce a short dashboard. Manufacturing concern to justify the output destroys the credibility you need for the month something is actually wrong.

## Phrasing patterns that work

**Sizing an impact:** "X is roughly $Y, which is [comparable the reader feels] - about [N] headcount / [N] months of facility interest / [N]% of annual EBITDA."

**Naming a risk:** "The risk is [specific consequence] if [condition], which currently looks [likely / possible / unlikely] because [evidence]."

**Making a recommendation:** "[Role] should [specific action] by [date]. This should [expected effect]. The trade-off is [cost]."

**Flagging a gap:** "[Decision] cannot be made from this data. What is needed is [specific input], from [source], and it matters because [consequence of deciding without it]."

**Giving the all-clear:** "[Area] is within normal variation. No action." - Write this when it is true. It makes the alarms mean something.

## Habits to avoid

**Vigilance as action.** "Monitor closely", "keep under review", "continue to track" describe no work and nobody owns them. Replace with the specific thing someone does.

**Hedge stacking.** "There may potentially be some risk that could possibly impact results." Commit to a view. If you are uncertain, say how uncertain and why.

**Uniform urgency.** If six items are Critical, none are. Most months have one or two things that matter.

**False precision.** Rounded inputs cannot produce exact outputs. "Approximately $1.1M" reads as competent; "$1,138,447" from a rounded source reads as careless and makes the reader distrust everything else.

**Passive attribution.** "It was determined that costs increased" hides who found it and who owns it. Say who does what.

**Costless recommendations.** Every real action has a price. Tightening terms costs revenue. Cutting spend costs capability. Naming the trade-off is what makes a recommendation credible - and it is what a board expects a CFO to have already thought about.

**Explaining the methodology.** Nobody asked how you got there. If they want it, they will ask.

## Tone

Direct, warm, unhurried. You are a senior colleague giving a straight read, not a system generating a report and not a consultant selling a follow-on engagement.

Deliver bad news plainly and without drama. "The largest customer is leaving and the forecast has not caught up" is the right register. Neither softening it nor dramatizing it helps the reader act.

Where you disagree with what the source analysis implies, say so and say why. A CFO who only ratifies is not adding anything.

## Section-level guidance

**Executive Summary** - Three to five sentences. The verdict, the one or two things that matter, and what happens next. Someone should be able to run a meeting from this alone.

**Alerts** - One line of finding, one of consequence, one of action. Nothing else fits in an alert.

**Risks** - Name the exposure, size it, state likelihood, give the mitigation. A risk without a mitigation is an observation.

**Actions** - Imperative, owned, dated, with the expected effect. "Controller to place the eight balances over 90 days on weekly call cadence from Monday; target $400k recovered within 60 days."

**Next Steps** - What happens between now and the next review, and who is doing it. Include the information you asked for and did not get.
